What it takes, honestly

Running your own insurance practice is real work, and it's yours to keep up. It's also very doable, one step at a time.

  • Months, not weeks. Each plan credentials you separately, and each usually takes three to nine months.
  • Paperwork that keeps going. Licenses, malpractice, CAQH re-attestation and recredentialing all renew on their own clocks.
  • Billing becomes your responsibility. It doesn't have to be hard: most practice software checks coverage, files claims and flags denials for you. You still own the follow-up.
  • Payment arrives later. Plans usually pay weeks after the session, so plan for the gap.

Many people start while still on a platform and move at their own pace. The sections below walk through it in order. We're working on free tools to help you keep track of applications, renewal dates and deadlines.

01 · Deciding

Is going independent right for you?

A platform does the credentialing, billing and chasing for you, and takes a share for it. On your own, you keep more of each session and take on that work yourself. Neither answer is wrong, and many therapists do both.

Questions worth answering first

  • How much income do you need each month, and could you cover the gap while the first claims are paid?
  • Which one or two plans do most of your current and future clients have? Starting small is common advice.
  • How much paperwork can you take on, or would you pay a biller?
  • Are you prepared to justify to a plan why a client needs more than about 20 sessions?There's no fixed limit, but plans expect focused work on symptoms, and some credentialing applications ask how many of your cases finish within about 20 sessions. Longer care is covered, even for years, when your treatment plan and notes show it's still needed, and a plan may ask to see them. Depth or growth work without symptom goals is the hardest to justify.
  • Would some clients follow you, and would their plans be ones you're contracted with?
  • Do you want to keep some work on a platform while you build up?

Staying on a platform, or keeping one alongside your own practice, is a good outcome too. This guide is for whoever wants to know what the other path looks like.

02 · The timeline

What happens, in order

Credentialing usually takes three to nine months per plan. Your in-network status on a platform belongs to the platform's contract with each plan, not to you, so it doesn't come with you. Most people start their own applications while still on the platform, so clients with those plans can follow without a gap.

Now
Get your identifiers in orderYour NPI (free from NPPES), a tax ID for the practice, malpractice coverage, and a complete, attested CAQH profile.
Months 0–9
Apply to plansKeep a log for each plan: who you spoke with, when, and the call reference number. If a panel says it's full, ask when to reapply. Six months is typical.
Contract signed
Record the detailsNote the effective date on the countersigned contract and keep a sheet of your contracted rate for each code and plan. Don't see clients under a plan before the effective date.
Before day one
Set up billing and paperworkChoose a clearinghouse or EHR, write your intake paperwork, and decide how you'll verify coverage.
Ongoing
Stay currentRe-attest CAQH on its schedule, recredential every two to three years, and never let a license or malpractice policy lapse.
03 · Before the first session

Check coverage before you see anyone

Find out what a plan covers before the first session, not from a denied claim weeks later. Mental health benefits are sometimes run by a different company than the one on the card, so confirm who handles them.

Ask the plan

Is the client covered?
  • Active on the session date, and are you in-network?
  • Any exclusions for your type of service?
What will they owe?
  • Copay or coinsurance
  • Deductible, and how much of it is left
  • Out-of-pocket maximum, and when the plan year resets
Rules for the service
  • Prior authorization or visit limits
  • Telehealth: covered, and which code and modifier the plan wants
Where the claim goes
  • Payer ID or mailing address for mental health claims
  • Log the call: date, name, reference number

Have in your paperwork

  • Who is responsible for unpaid balances
  • That a diagnosis goes to the plan, and the plan may review records
  • Your missed-appointment policy
  • A release for claims, and assignment of benefits
  • A question about secondary insurance
  • For self-pay clients: a Good Faith Estimate, given within 1 business day of scheduling (3 if the visit is 10 or more business days away)
Re-check benefits every January. Deductibles reset, and plans change with jobs.
04 · Documentation

Notes that hold up in a review

On a platform, the platform deals with the plan. On your own, your notes are your evidence if a plan asks for records or wants money back.

What a reviewer looks for
A billable diagnosis, symptoms and how they affect daily life, measurable goals, specific interventions tied to those goals, progress, and the risk picture. Standard measures like the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 help.
The details people miss
Write down how long the session really ran, because the billing code has to match it. Name the technique you used and how the client responded. Write notes within a day or two, and label a later one as a late entry. To correct a note, add a dated addendum and leave the original as it was.
Telehealth notes
You need a license where the client is during the session, so record their location every time. Note whether you met by video or phone, and that you confirmed who you were talking to. The first time, also record consent, why telehealth fits this client, and an emergency plan for where they are.
Psychotherapy notes
Keep process notes separate from the record. They don't go to plans, even in an audit.

Rules vary by plan and change over time. Treat this as a starting point and confirm with each payer.

When a claim comes back denied

Start with the reason code on the remittance. Most denials fall into one of three kinds, and each has a different fix.

Something on the claim was wrong or missingA code, a date, the client's member ID, the other insurance. Fix it and send a corrected claim, marked as a replacement rather than a new one.
Authorization was missing or ran outAsk the plan whether it will authorize after the fact, then resubmit with the authorization number.
The plan says it wasn't medically necessaryAsk to speak with the plan's reviewing clinician. If that doesn't settle it, appeal in writing with notes that show symptoms, how they affect daily life, and progress toward goals.

If the plan still says no, the denial notice explains how to file an appeal and the deadline. After the plan's own appeal, most plans have to offer an independent external review, and your state's insurance department can help with plans it regulates.

Sources: HealthCare.gov, appealing a health plan decision · State insurance departments (NAIC)

06 · Staying compliant

Bill exactly what happened

A claim says who you saw, what you did, when, where, for how long, and what it cost. Keep every one of those true and most billing trouble never starts.

  • The code matches the minutes on the clock
  • The place of service matches where the client actually was
  • The date is the day the session happened
  • The diagnosis is one your notes support
  • What the client pays follows your contract and your written policy, including copays
  • The original note stays as written; changes are dated additions

More in the HHS Office of Inspector General's compliance guidance, written for practices of every size.

07 · The transition

Leaving a platform well

  • Read your platform agreement for the notice period, any terms about clients, and how to get copies of records
  • Find out what you can export before you give notice. Access to notes you wrote on a platform can end when you leave, and notes can matter years later, so keep your own copy if your agreement allows it.
  • Tell each client their options, including staying with the platform and another clinician
  • Line up start dates so there's no gap in coverage, license or malpractice
